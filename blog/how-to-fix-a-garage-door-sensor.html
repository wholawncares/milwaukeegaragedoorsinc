How to fix a garage door sensor

A blinking or misaligned garage door sensor stops the door closing. Here is how to check, clean, and align the sensors safely, and when the problem needs a technician.

Milwaukee Garage Doors IncNovember 12, 20269 min read

Garage door sensors are the small photo-eye units near the floor on either side of the door. They send a beam across the doorway, and if the beam is broken while the door is closing, the door stops and reverses. When they fail, the door usually refuses to close at all, which is the system working as designed: it will not close blind.

This guide covers what the sensors do, why they blink, the checks a homeowner can safely do, and the point at which to call a technician. No brand names and no model-specific instructions appear here; blink patterns and indicator behavior vary by opener, so your opener manual governs the details.

What a garage door sensor does and why it fails

Federal rules (16 CFR Part 1211, UL 325) require residential automatic garage door operators to have entrapment protection: an inherent reversal system plus a secondary entrapment protection device (such as photoelectric sensors or an edge sensor) or a constant-contact control. Test sensors and auto-reverse regularly. The sensors are the photoelectric half of that protection: one unit sends the beam, the other receives it, and the opener refuses to close the door if the beam is not reaching the receiver.

Sensors fail in a handful of ways. The lenses get dirty or fogged, the units get knocked out of alignment, wiring works loose or is damaged, sunlight or reflections interfere with the beam, or a unit itself fails. Cold weather can make alignment and lens problems show up more noticeably, as metal contracts and lenses fog or ice over, though that is general guidance rather than a measured pattern for Zion.

The key symptom is a door that will not close, or closes partway and reverses, while the opener itself runs. A door that only closes when the wall button is held down is usually showing a sensor fault: the hold-to-close behavior is an override, and it should not be treated as a routine way to run the door. Fix the sensor fault instead.

Why is my garage door sensor blinking in Zion?

A blinking sensor light usually means misalignment, an obstruction, or a wiring problem. The sending unit and the receiving unit each have an indicator light, and their behavior differs: typically one stays steady when powered and the other shows whether the beam is aligned. The exact meaning of each blink pattern varies by opener model, so no blink-code table is given here; check your opener manual for the pattern your unit shows.

Start with the simple causes before assuming a failed unit. Check for an object blocking the beam path, dirt or cobwebs on either lens, and units that have been bumped out of line. Check that both units are firmly mounted and pointing at each other. If the lights behave differently after cleaning and realigning, the problem was likely alignment or dirt.

If the lights stay dark on one unit with power to the opener, or if blinking continues after the beam path is clear and the units are aligned, the cause may be wiring or a failed unit. Wiring work and unit replacement belong to a technician; see garage door opener repair in Zion, IL, which covers sensor work as part of opener service.

How do I fix my garage door sensor in Zion IL?

Work through these checks in order, with the door in a safe state. None of them involves springs, cables, or track hardware.

  1. Clear the beam path. Remove anything sitting in the doorway near the floor, including stored items, debris, and snow or ice after storms.
  2. Clean both lenses. Wipe each lens gently with a soft, dry cloth. Remove cobwebs and grime from around the units without forcing them out of position.
  3. Check the mounting. Confirm both units are firmly attached to the track brackets and have not been knocked loose. Tighten a loose bracket by hand if it is simply loose, without forcing the unit.
  4. Realign the units. Loosen the mounting wing nut or bracket screw just enough to aim the unit, point it squarely at the unit opposite, and retighten. Watch the indicator light: it should steady when the beam lines up. Adjust one unit at a time.
  5. Check the wiring visually. Look for loose, pinched, or damaged wires running to each unit. Do not strip, splice, or reroute wiring yourself; note what you see and pass it to a technician if the problem continues.
  6. Test the door. Run the door through a full close with the wall control, then test the safety reverse and the beam interruption: wave an object through the beam while the door closes and confirm the door stops and reverses.

If the door now closes and reverses correctly, the fault was dirt or alignment. If it still will not close, or if the lights show a fault you cannot clear, stop and call a technician. Do not disconnect, bypass, or tape over the sensors to force the door to run; that removes a required safety feature.

Garage door sensor alignment: getting the beam true

Alignment is a frequent sensor fix, and it is a matter of small adjustments. Both units must face each other squarely across the doorway at the same height. A unit tilted up, down, or sideways by a small amount can miss the receiver entirely.

Adjust in small steps. Move one unit slightly, watch its indicator light, and stop when the light steadies. Then check the other unit the same way. If the light flickers as the door vibrates during travel, the mounting may be loose or the bracket bent, and a technician should look at the bracket and track.

Sunlight can interfere with the beam at certain times of day, particularly for doors facing the sun. If the fault appears only at one time of day and clears at others, shading the receiving unit or adjusting its angle slightly may help, but do not cover the lens. If the pattern persists, ask a technician; the fix may be a repositioned unit or a different sensor arrangement suited to the door.

After any alignment work, always retest the safety reverse and the beam interruption before returning the door to regular use. A door that closes is not enough; it must also stop and reverse when the beam is broken.

Common mistakes to avoid

  1. Bypassing the sensors. Disconnecting, taping over, or wiring around the sensors removes a required safety feature. Never treat a bypass as a fix.
  2. Forcing the alignment. Bent brackets and forced units break or drift out of line again. Small adjustments, firmly retightened, hold better.
  3. Using the hold-to-close override routinely. Holding the wall button to close a door with a sensor fault is an override for an emergency, not a daily routine. Fix the fault.
  4. Cleaning with harsh chemicals or abrasives. A soft, dry cloth is enough for the lenses. Harsh cleaners can cloud or damage them.
  5. Assuming the opener is dead. A door that will not close with a running opener is usually reporting a sensor, force, or balance problem, not a dead opener. Check the sensors first.

When a sensor problem needs a technician

Call a technician when cleaning and alignment do not clear the fault, when wiring looks damaged or loose, when a unit shows no light with power to the opener, when the bracket or track is bent, or when the door still will not reverse correctly after the sensors are aligned. Sensor work that involves wiring, unit replacement, or opener settings belongs to a technician.

What sensor work involves depends on the cause: realignment and cleaning are the simplest outcomes, while wiring repair or unit replacement adds parts and opener-side checks. If the door will not close and the sensors cannot be cleared, the door should be left in its safe state and the opener not forced. Describe the indicator lights and what you have already checked when you call; that helps the technician bring the right parts.

When to call a professional

Call a professional when the door will not close after cleaning and alignment, when sensor lights stay dark or keep blinking, when wiring is damaged, when the safety reverse fails its test, or when the door only closes while the wall button is held down. Each of those means the safety system is not working as required.
